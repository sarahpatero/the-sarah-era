I’m the happiest person you will ever meet, and it’s because I think about death all the time.


Nothing is certain in this life except for one thing: we are going to die. She could creep up on me right now as I’m writing this article in the comfort of my own bedroom. Or she could stop me in my tracks tomorrow when I ride a motorcycle with my friend. Or she could finally lay me to rest in 70 years, after a rich, fruitful life. The only certain thing in an incredibly uncertain life is that it ends, and that’s the thing that makes me want to live mine as loud and as full as I possibly can.


Every morning, and multiple times a day, I remind myself that somebody didn’t wake up today. Somebody, just like me, took their last breath while I was still asleep, safe, and here. I sit with those thoughts and ask myself, what separates me from them?


Nothing separates me from the 20-year old girl who also had big dreams and aspirations, who died in a fatal car crash on her way to the gym this morning. I just happened to still be here, by the grace of God or of sheer luck.


I remind myself often that I am not entitled to my life. My life was a gift that I didn’t ask for. I didn’t do anything to deserve this gift, and it’s not always in my control how this gift gets taken away. As a stranger I met in Seattle said to me on my first solo-trip as a young female: “You can do your best to avoid the danger, but the danger doesn’t care about avoiding you.”


I am not entitled to my life. And that thought keeps me so overwhelmingly grateful. I haven’t lost anyone close to me yet, and I know that’s not something I earned, that’s just luck running my way so far, and luck runs out for everyone eventually. Death comes for everyone. Illness sneaks up on you when you least expect it. Hardship could knock on your door in one hour.


I do this thing where I scan a room, or a trail, or a random parking lot I’ve never been to, and I think, somewhere in some other version of this exact moment, something happened to someone standing right where I’m standing. Somebody was just sitting alone in a park in a state they’d never been to before, same as me, and something took them. What made that not me? Nothing did. That’s not a scary thought to me anymore, it’s a grounding one. It reminds me I’m not entitled to any of this. Not my next breath, not this view, not this conversation, none of it. I don’t own any of it. I just get to borrow it for a while.


So I try to walk around treating everything like it might be the last time I see it. Last time I see this tree. Last time I have this conversation. Last time I get to hear this person’s laugh. And when you actually live like that, you stop caring about the petty stuff. The little arguments, the small dramas, none of it survives contact with the thought that this could be your last day. You just want to be present. You want to notice things. You want to look at people like they matter, because they do, because maybe today is their last day too and they don’t even know it, and the least I can do is make them feel like they matter while they’re here.


People go looking for meaning like it’s hiding somewhere far away, when it’s honestly just sitting right in front of them the whole time. The trees. The people you get to talk to today. Your own heartbeat. It’s all right there. You just have to actually look.


Talk to people like it’s the last conversation you’ll ever have with them. Not in a dramatic way, just in a way where you actually mean it when you ask how they’re doing. Because somebody out there is having their actual last day today, and they have no idea, and neither do you, and neither do I.
