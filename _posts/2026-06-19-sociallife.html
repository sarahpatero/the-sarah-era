My social life has never been better, and all that changed was that I started working 40+ hours a week. Before, I had endless free time in the summer. But when you can do anything, it's harder to find yourself doing what actually matters.


Economists call this the scarcity principle. When a resource is limited, you stop taking it for granted and start allocating it carefully. Think about how differently you spend the last twenty dollars in your wallet versus twenty dollars in a full bank account. Time works the same way; now that I have maybe four good hours on a weekday, I treat them like they're sacred. I've split how I spend my leisure time into three intentional categories that replenish, instead of drain, my energy after a long work day:


  1. Spending time in nature
  2. Working out and staying active
  3. Spending quality time with people I love

I especially focus on #3. Psychology has long established that humans are fundamentally social animals. This goes back to Maslow's hierarchy, where love and belonging sit right above basic survival needs, but the research runs much deeper than that.


Social connection is one of the strongest predictors of wellbeing we have. Studies consistently link strong relationships to lower cortisol levels, reduced risk of depression and anxiety, better immune function, and even longer life expectancy. The Harvard Study of Adult Development, one of the longest running studies on human happiness ever conducted, found that the quality of our relationships was the single greatest predictor of how well we age, physically and emotionally.


Relationships. Not wealth. Not status. Not career achievement.


Now I schedule time with friends and the people I love one week in advance. I know that sounds overly planned, maybe even clinical, because friendships are supposed to be "spontaneous". But I've found the opposite to be true. Intentionality is a form of respect, and when I put someone in my calendar, I am telling them and myself that this matters. When our plans happen by choice instead of accident, there's something about that deliberate choice that makes the time together feel even richer.


I am lucky to have great friends. People who show up, who make me laugh, who I can be completely myself around. And every single time I am with them, I leave more energized than when I arrived. We are wired for connection in a way that no amount of alone time, scrolling, or passive entertainment can replicate. Human presence does something for us that nothing else can.


When your time is scarce, you stop drifting and start choosing. And when you start choosing, you almost always choose people.


As an actuarial math, economics, and psychology triple major, I believe the best insights live in the overlap between multiple disciplines.
