My first big girl purchase was a solo trip to Seattle at 20 years old. Fully paid for by myself, planned on my own, with no one in Seattle waiting for me.


Why Seattle, why even solo trip?


It seemingly came out of nowhere. I looked up on Reddit “nice nature vacation spots in the USA”, saw one pic of Seattle, booked my flight the next day. Three weeks out. I thought you’re supposed to plan out a vacation for months. I’d been wanting to vacation by myself for awhile, but I have one specific moment where all of a sudden, the need to solo vacation overwhelmed me, and seized my consciousness until I took action.


One month earlier, I was in Helsinki, Finland. I told my parents I was going down the block to buy a skirt from the vendors in the city square. They unnecessarily freaked out, and said “no, no, no! You can’t go alone. That is too dangerous! You might get hurt!” There I was, a 20-year old woman, being talked to like I was 5. I felt belittled. Do my parents seriously still see me like this?


I walked out of the hotel to the city square anyway. On the walk there, I thought so clearly to myself, “I need to go on a solo trip. I’m completely capable and ready to be somewhere on my own. I need to show my parents that I am an adult.”


In my developmental psychology class, I learned about how the transition from adolescence to adulthood has evolved to include another phase in between: emerging adulthood. I was legally an adult, but admittedly, I did not feel like one. I couldn’t completely blame my parents either for still seeing me as a child because in some ways, I still felt like one. I knew a solo trip would break me out of that shell, and give me a taste of what feeling like an adult actually is like.


I have to take a moment to be grateful that I am able to be this spontaneous. I’ve built myself up to a financial position where I can just book a flight out of nowhere, without my parents knowing, and also somehow expanded my audacity far enough to actually follow through and not just let that thought dissipate into nothingness.


I have strict Asian parents. I imagined that if they found out, they would lock me in the house, guarding every exit to make sure I have no chance of leaving. So I just didn’t tell them until I was already on the flight. That way I had to pay for everything myself: flights, Airbnb, tours. And this meant a lot more to me than if I just spent my parents’ money. By buying everything myself with my own money, as a college student with a measly income, I showed myself that I am worth spending big money on, I am worth investing in, I am worth the best experiences I can give myself. It taught me a deep sense of self-love because you spend big money on things you care about. This wasn’t going to be just a “fun” vacation. I knew this was going to be a period of profound self-expansion, starting with this direct investment into myself.


Side track. Women have been bombarded with the messaging of MEN SHOULD SPOIL YOU AND PROVIDE FOR YOU. As a girly girl that is attracted to men who provide, I believe that you can only be healthily attracted to partners who provide IF AND ONLY IF YOU FIRST are capable of providing that for yourself. Then, the provision from another person won’t come from a place of desperation, because you know you can do it all for yourself too. Investing in yourself shows other people how they should invest in you too, and how you won’t accept anything less than. Because why would you be with someone who can’t provide a better life for you than you can for yourself?


The novelty.


It goes without saying. The research is so clear. Novelty is incredible for our brains and levels of happiness. And going on a solo vacation for the first time might just be the most novel thing you can do. All for the first time all at once, you have a new home, new street you live on, new grocery store you go to, new weather, new gym, new library, new job, and don’t even get me started on the people. Every single person you meet is a novel experience.


Novelty isn’t just good for our brains because it’s the spice of life, but the newness teaches us how to handle uncertainty with confidence. Humans hate to be uncertain about something, that’s why we can’t help but to make assumptions. Nothing is certain, and especially so when you are on your vacation. Anything can happen. This vacation taught me how to tackle uncertainty with grace, and built a greater sense of competence within myself.


The self-trust.


You’re on your own schedule where you could completely do whatever you want. I had to trust myself to give myself a great vacation.


You handle your finances by yourself. It’s such a big shift spending your own money instead of your parents’. The exact shift that helped me stop feeling like the child that my parents still saw me as in Helsinki, Finland. It feels freeing because you quietly know that you are capable of providing for yourself. And it feels like a direct investment into yourself. Into your own happiness and care.


You’re forced to talk to strangers.


How I didn’t just put myself out there, but rocket launched myself into conversations multiple times a day was the highlight of my trip. I can’t tell you about every stop we had on my national park tours, but I can tell you the names of every stranger I met and about the conversations we had. This meant so much to me, that I literally started posting on LinkedIn about sociality, as an actuarial math student. I was that passionate about this part of my trip, and it sparked in me an intense passion for human connection, especially with strangers.


Every bond that exists between two people can only exist once in the entirety of the universe. A bond between two specific people has a 1 in a trillion chance of occurring. Isn’t that exciting? Every new person you meet opens up a portal you can never step through with anyone else, ever again. That’s the attitude I led with in Seattle. I saw every person as special as they are.


I think about this a lot: what kind of person spontaneously strikes up a conversation with a stranger? Someone confident and not paralyzed by the possibility of rejection, and regulated enough to be genuinely present with another human being.


The small talk was never meant to become a lifelong friendship. But the good conversations, the ones where two people just sync, leave an afterglow. A quiet sweetness in your chest. A restored faith in the human condition. And here’s what I’ve noticed: when you’re enthusiastic, ask good questions, and smile, you’re naturally going to meet people who leave you feeling energized.


One of my catchphrases is “novelty is the spice of life”. And what’s more novel than a person you’ve never met?


You have to test your own courage.


I’ve touched on how going on a solo vacation and how talking to strangers are whole domains of courage in themselves. There are smaller ways I had to test my own courage on this trip. And I’m thinking about the most emotional moment of my life: sitting at the summit of Mount Si while listening to “Let Down” by Radiohead as tears uncontrollably cascaded down my face.


20 minutes into the hike, I realized that I severely underestimated how challenging this would be. There I was, looking at my Strava app that said I’d only climbed 300 ft, and I had 4,000 more to go. I was unimaginably out of breath and fatigued. As I kept ascending, I considered turning around numerous times. “There is absolutely no way I can make it to the top.” With how exhausted I already was, I didn’t know if I had it in me to keep going.


You enjoy your alone time.


Luckily I’m not plagued by the need to never be alone. I thoroughly enjoy my solitude. Which made this vacation feel refreshing. Uninterrupted, undivided time with myself. Not having to work around anyone else’s wants or feelings, only focused on what I want.


I noticed that my mind ran rampant. As the days became abysmally quiet, I found myself only thinking thoughts that are genuinely mine; my regular world’s input had lessened.


And the best part: there is no version of you people expect you to be. No one knows who you are. You can start completely fresh. I got to see who I was without bearing in mind who I already am to others. You learn who you are when there isn't an audience.


Home.


So not to be superfluously dramatic or anything, but home doesn’t have to be a physical place on Earth. Home is cultivated within you when you feel safe in your own skin. You are your own home. And Seattle internalized that message to me. Never in my mind had I ever conceived the idea of travelling to Seattle. But when I was there, I instantly felt at home. Was it Seattle itself? Beautiful, breathtaking city, but the feeling of home was actually me feeling safe in my own body.


Alone in a city where nobody knew me. I felt free to be completely myself, by myself. Sitting on top of the Mount Si summit, listening to “Let Down” by Radiohead. Tears flowing down my face like a never-ending river. Laying down on Secret Beach under the moonlight on my last night, listening to more “Let Down” by Radiohead. Stalling as to not say goodbye to the last night in a city that stole a piece of me. I had never felt more at home. In a city I’d never been to, never dreamed of, and never considered going to until three weeks before my flight departed. Because here in this brand new city, I felt my peace come alive. I felt so safe by myself, walking these streets, taking the public transportation, eating in new places, and talking to new people everyday. I now yearn to move back to a place that I never knew existed, and now feel sorely attached to.


I wonder if the air still carries my scent. I remember Seattle everyday, does Seattle remember me? It has to, the way it found me on a whim. Will the wind wrap around me like a sweet, welcoming embrace? Will the ground swallow me full as a means to never let me leave again? I mean to be exactly as dramatic as this trip deserves.
